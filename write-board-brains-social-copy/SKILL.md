# Write Board Brains Social Copy

Turn a Board Brains video transcript into supporting copy without letting the copy outrun the video. Establish shared understanding first, agree on a framing second, and write only after both approval gates.

Read [references/board-brains-editorial.md](references/board-brains-editorial.md) before analysing a transcript or writing copy.

## Required input

Require a transcript. Use any purpose, audience, campaign context, or constraints supplied by the user, but do not require the user to provide information that can be reasonably inferred from the transcript.

If no transcript is available, ask for it and stop. Do not invent the video's substance from a title or short summary.

## Workflow

Follow the three stages in order. Treat the end of stages 1 and 2 as hard approval gates. Do not collapse or skip a gate unless the user explicitly asks to do so.

### Stage 1: Understand and align

Read the complete transcript. Distinguish the video's literal subject from its more interesting tension, question, opinion, transformation, or viewer benefit.

Present two plausible transcript-grounded options for each alignment point by default:

1. **Purpose and value propositions**: What the video is trying to achieve for the creator or viewer, and what viewers gain by watching, including practical, emotional, social, or intellectual value.
2. **Audience**: Who is most likely to care and what makes the video relevant to them.
3. **Themes and central topics**: The larger ideas, concrete subjects, and interesting tension points the video materially covers.

Keep the entire stage compact, targeting about 250 words unless genuine ambiguity requires more. Offer a third option only when it would materially change the eventual framing. Do not pad the list with weak paraphrases merely to increase the count.

For each option:

- State it in one concise sentence.
- Include the shortest supporting transcript detail inline, preferably as a brief parenthetical rather than a separate evidence line or quotation.
- Keep it distinct from the other options.
- Do not repeat the same idea under multiple alignment points.

Recommend the strongest option or combination in no more than two sentences. Treat the recommendation as a proposal, not a conclusion. Then propose a concise **reveal boundary** with:

- **Safe to disclose**: The premise and context the supporting copy may use.
- **Save for the video**: The identities, results, verdicts, rankings, recommendations, or other payoffs viewers should discover by watching.

Ask the user to select, combine, revise, or reject the options. Stop without drafting hooks, captions, titles, or framing directions.

### Stage 2: Explore and align on framing

After stage 1 is approved, develop at least two genuinely different framing directions from the agreed purpose and value propositions, audience, themes and central topics, and reveal boundary.

For each direction, state:

- **Lead**: The tension, question, contrast, or practical need that captures attention.
- **Promise and payoff**: What expectation the framing creates and where the transcript sincerely fulfils it.
- **Disclosure check**: Why the framing preserves what viewers should discover in the video.
- **Closing prompt**: The specific conversation the caption should invite.

Prefer the most specific personal tension, useful insight, surprising contrast, or unresolved question. Do not default to a listicle or generic summary merely because it is easy to describe.

Recommend one direction when the evidence supports it. Ask the user to select, combine, revise, or reject the directions. Stop without drafting final copy.

### Stage 3: Write the final copy

Proceed only after the framing is approved. Produce exactly these default artifacts:

1. **Primary hook**: One sentence or short phrase expressing the approved framing.
2. **Caption**: One concise, platform-agnostic caption whose first sentence is the primary hook verbatim.
3. **Video title variations**: Three variations by default, adapting the hook to balance curiosity with clearer description for platforms such as YouTube.

Do not include alternative hooks, platform-specific captions, hashtags, keywords, thumbnail text, pinned comments, or other metadata unless requested.

## Writing rules

### Reveal boundary

Preserve anything viewers are meant to discover by watching, not only the final conclusion. Apply the approved reveal boundary to the hook, caption, and title variations.

Use these format-specific defaults:

- **Multi-item list, ranking, or roundup**: Refer to the category, premise, or number of items when useful, but do not name or enumerate the individual items in the hook or caption. Do not reveal their order, ranking, winner, or final recommendations. Apply the same boundary to titles unless the user explicitly chooses otherwise.
- **Single-game video**: Name the game because it is the premise, but withhold the verdict, result, strongest finding, or other payoff.
- **Comparison**: Name the subjects when they are the stated premise, but withhold the winner or final preference when that is part of the payoff.
- **Experiment or challenge**: State the setup, but withhold the result.

Cross a default boundary only when the user explicitly approves it or the transcript makes clear that the information is setup rather than payoff.

### Hook

- Capture attention with a sincere promise that the video actually pays off.
- Use assumptions, contrast, ambiguity, or an unexpected framing when supported by the transcript.
- Preserve the video's unresolved question or tension.
- Stay within the approved reveal boundary.
- Avoid inflated stakes and conventional social-media clickbait unless the exaggeration is intentionally self-aware and clearly supported.
- Make the hook strong enough to work in isolation.

### Caption

- Repeat the primary hook verbatim as the first sentence.
- Add only the context needed to understand why the premise matters.
- Develop the approved tension instead of summarising every section of the video.
- Stay within the approved reveal boundary and preserve the reason to watch.
- End every caption with a specific, natural participation prompt that continues the conversation opened by the video.
- Prefer an easy-to-answer question. Use a direct invitation only when it sounds more natural.
- Invite experience, preference, advice, prediction, or reflection instead of generic engagement such as "Thoughts?" or "Do you agree?"
- Keep the copy easy to scan. Treat it as secondary to the video.

### Video title variations

- Preserve the central promise of the approved hook.
- Add concrete subject matter when it improves clarity or search comprehension.
- Keep each title faithful to the transcript and distinct in emphasis.
- Stay within the approved reveal boundary.
- Avoid turning the title into a complete synopsis.

## Final check

Before returning final copy, verify that:

- The hook's promise is paid off in the transcript.
- The caption begins with the exact primary hook.
- The hook, caption, and titles stay within the approved reveal boundary.
- A multi-item video does not disclose its individual items unless the user explicitly approved that choice.
- The caption adds context without reproducing the video's payoff.
- The caption ends with a specific prompt that relates directly to the video.
- The copy reflects the approved purpose and value propositions, audience, themes and central topics, and framing.
- The language sounds casual, friendly, direct, and human.
- Any cheekiness feels natural rather than inserted by formula.
- No em dash appears.
- No unsupported fact, experience, or opinion has been invented.
- Only the three default artifacts appear unless the user requested more.
